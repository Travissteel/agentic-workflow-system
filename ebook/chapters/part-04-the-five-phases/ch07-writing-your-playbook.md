# Chapter 7: Mastering the Strategic Directive

## Business Engineering vs. Manual Labor

If the Orchestrator is your General and the Specialist Agents are your soldiers, the **Directive** is your Mission Order.

In traditional, inefficient businesses, these are called SOPs (Standard Operating Procedures). But let’s be honest: most SOPs are where good ideas go to die. They are 20-page Google Docs that nobody reads, buried in a folder that nobody can find, referencing software that was replaced three years ago.

In the Antigravity ecosystem, we don't write "procedures." We **engineer logic**.

A Directive is a precision instrument. It is the bridge between your high-level business strategy and the deterministic execution of an AI agent. It is the difference between telling an employee to "handle leads better" and architecting a system that qualifies, enriches, and routes 1,000 leads with zero human intervention.

The goal of Phase 2 is to extract the tribal knowledge from your head and codify it into a Strategic Directive. We are moving from "hope" as a strategy to "logic" as a result.

## The Paradigm Shift: Probabilistic vs. Deterministic

To master the DOE framework, you must understand the two layers of any automated workflow:

1. **The Probabilistic Layer (The Directive)**: This is written in Markdown. It uses natural language to define the "what" and the "why." Because it’s interpreted by Gemini, it can handle nuance, context, and edge cases that would break traditional code.
2. **The Deterministic Layer (The Execution)**: This is the Python or JavaScript code generated by your subagents. It is rigid, fast, and does the heavy lifting.

**The Orchestrator’s Secret:** You don’t need to write the code. You only need to master the Directive. If your logic is flawless, the AI will build the execution layer for you. If your logic is messy, the AI will automate your mess.

## Anatomy of a High-Performance Directive

Every mission-critical Directive consists of four non-negotiable components. If you miss one, the system reverts to "guessing"—and guessing is the precursor to failure.

### 1. The Strategic Objective

The objective is your North Star. It answers one question: "What is the exact state of the world when this mission is complete?"

Vague objectives create vague results. Precision creates profit.

- **The Amateur:** "Get me some real estate leads."
- **The Orchestrator:** "Identify 50 real estate agencies in Melbourne, extract the Head of Growth's contact data, and verify they have at least 10 active listings. Format output as a CSV in `./output/leads.csv`."

**The Test:** If you handed this objective to a temp worker with no context, could they deliver exactly what you want? If not, keep refining.

### 2. Input Specifications (The Logistics)

Your agents can’t cook without ingredients. You must define every data source, every credential, and every constraint before the mission begins.

**The Vault (.env)**: Never put secrets in a directive. We store API keys in the `.env` file and reference them using `.env[KEY_NAME]`. This keeps your business logic portable and your credentials secure.

### 3. The Execution Path (Step-by-Step)

This is where you map the logic. Each step should be a single, verifiable action. Use active, outcome-driven verbs: *Extract, Validate, Cross-reference, Route, Escalate.*

**The "Stuck" Protocol**: Real business isn't linear. You must build branch points. If the AI hits an unknown scenario, your directive tells it to invoke the `stuck` agent. This keeps a "Human-in-the-loop" for critical decisions while the machine handles the 95% of routine execution.

### 4. Definition of Done (The Success Metric)

Success isn't "the agent stopped running." Success is a verifiable outcome.

Your Definition of Done is the **Tester’s Checklist**. If a single item on this list isn't met, the mission is a failure.
- *Output exists at X location.*
- *Data format matches Y template.*
- *Verification email sent to Z.*

#### The "Conversation First" Approach

If writing a structured Directive feels intimidating, don't worry. You don't need to be a technical writer or a programmer. The beauty of working with AI is that you can simply describe your process in plain English, and the AI will help you structure it.

Here's how this conversation typically unfolds:

**You:** "Here's how I handle my invoicing. I get invoices by email, I check if the vendor is in my system, I verify the amount matches the PO, then I approve it in QuickBooks."

**AI:** "Got it. Let me structure that as a directive for you. Here's what I've created: [shows structured directive]. Does this look right? I have some questions: What email address receives invoices? What happens if the vendor isn't in your system? What's the approval threshold before a human needs to sign off?"

**You:** "Oh, anything over $5,000 needs my approval."

**AI:** "Perfect, I've added that to the error handling rules. So to confirm: invoices under $5,000 get auto-approved if all validation checks pass, and invoices over $5,000 get flagged for your manual review?"

**You:** "Exactly."

Notice what happened here. You started with a rough description, and the AI helped you discover the gaps in your own process. You didn't realize you had an approval threshold until the AI asked. This is one of the hidden benefits of writing Directives - it forces you to think through edge cases you've been handling intuitively.

The AI will do the heavy lifting of structure; you just provide the expertise. Think of it as having a business analyst interview you about your process, except the analyst never gets tired, never forgets details, and produces a perfectly formatted document at the end.

### The 4 Essential Components

Every successful Directive contains four specific sections. If you miss even one, the AI will start to "guess" - and as we learned in Part 3, we never want the AI to guess.

#### Component 1: Clear Objective

The objective is your north star. It answers one simple question: "What are we trying to accomplish?"

Without a clear objective, the AI doesn't know what "success" looks like. It will produce SOMETHING, but not necessarily what you wanted. This is like hiring a contractor and saying "build something nice" instead of "build a 3-bedroom house with oak floors and a south-facing deck."

**The Specificity Test:**

Let's look at three versions of the same objective, each increasing in precision:

- **Bad:** "Generate leads" → Too vague. What kind of leads? How many? From where? The AI could scrape email addresses from a spam list and technically complete this objective.

- **Better:** "Find 50 real estate agents in Brisbane" → Clear target. The AI knows the quantity (50), the profession (real estate agents), and the location (Brisbane).

- **Best:** "Find 50 real estate agents in Brisbane with active listings in the last 30 days, extract their name, email, phone, and agency name into a CSV" → Precise, measurable, verifiable. The AI knows exactly what data to collect, how to qualify the targets, and what format to deliver.

**Common Mistakes:**

1. **Too vague:** "Improve our marketing" - Improve how? What metrics? By how much?
2. **Too broad:** "Build a complete CRM system" - This is a 6-month project, not a single directive
3. **Too technical:** "Implement a RESTful CRUD API with JWT auth" - You're describing HOW instead of WHAT
4. **Just right:** "Build a contact form that saves submissions to Supabase and sends a notification email" - Clear outcome, specific enough to execute

Think of your objective as the one-sentence answer to "What will exist at the end of this that doesn't exist now?" If you can't answer that clearly, the AI can't either.

#### Component 2: Input Specifications

The AI can't work with data it doesn't have. If you forget to specify an input, the AI will either guess (bad) or get stuck (better, but wastes time).

Input specifications answer: "What do we need before we can start?"

**Categories of inputs:**

1. **Data sources:** URLs to scrape, CSV files to process, API endpoints to call, database tables to query
2. **Credentials:** API keys, passwords, tokens (more on secure storage in a moment)
3. **Configuration:** Output format preferences, naming conventions, target platforms, rate limits
4. **Constraints:** Budget caps, time windows, quality thresholds

Here's a complete input specification for a real-world lead scraper:

```markdown
## Inputs
- Target URL: https://www.realestate.com.au/find-agent/
- Location filter: Brisbane, QLD
- Output file: ./output/brisbane-agents.csv
- Output format: CSV with columns [Name, Email, Phone, Agency, Suburb, Active Listings]
- Rate limit: Maximum 1 request per 2 seconds (respect robots.txt)
- Credentials: None required (public directory)
- Constraints: Only include agents with 5+ active listings
- Date range: Active listings in last 30 days only
```

Notice the level of detail. The AI knows exactly where to look, what to extract, how fast to scrape, and how to filter results. There's no room for interpretation.

**The .env File: Credential Management**

Here's a critical security principle: NEVER put passwords or API keys directly in your Directive file.

Instead, you create a special file called `.env` that stores your secrets. Your Directive references them by name, like this: `.env[HUBSPOT_KEY]`

Here's why this matters:

- **Security:** The .env file is added to .gitignore, which means it never leaves your computer and never gets committed to version control
- **Sharing:** When you share your Directive with a team member or client, you don't accidentally expose your credentials
- **Flexibility:** Different team members can use their own API keys without modifying the Directive

Here's what a .env file looks like:

```
HUBSPOT_KEY=abc123xyz789
RESEND_API_KEY=re_1234567890
SUPABASE_URL=https://yourproject.supabase.co
SUPABASE_KEY=eyJhbGciOiJIUzI1NiIsInR5cCI6IkpXVCJ9...
```

And here's how you reference it in your Directive:

```markdown
## Inputs
- HubSpot API Key: .env[HUBSPOT_KEY]
- Email API Key: .env[RESEND_API_KEY]
- Database URL: .env[SUPABASE_URL]
```

The AI knows to look up these values at runtime without ever exposing them in the Directive itself.

#### Component 3: Step-by-Step Process

This is the meat of the Directive. If the objective is "where we're going" and inputs are "what we need," the process is "how we get there."

The process section is where most people struggle, because it requires thinking through every detail of a task you might normally do on autopilot. But this is also where the magic happens - when you write out your process step-by-step, you often discover inefficiencies or gaps you never noticed before.

**Writing Effective Steps:**

Each step should be ONE action, not three actions crammed together. Use specific, verifiable verbs like:
- Navigate
- Extract
- Save
- Validate
- Send
- Create
- Update
- Check

Avoid vague verbs that leave room for interpretation:
- Handle (handle how?)
- Process (process how?)
- Manage (what does this mean?)
- Deal with (too ambiguous)

**Bad vs Good Steps:**

**Bad:** "Process the invoice and put it in the system"

This tells the AI nothing. What does "process" mean? What system? How should it be entered?

**Good:**
```markdown
1. Open the invoice PDF from the email attachment
2. Extract the following fields: vendor name, invoice number, date, line items, total amount
3. Validate that total amount matches sum of line items (tolerance: $0.01)
4. Match vendor name against the vendors table in Supabase
5. If vendor found: create invoice record with status "Pending Approval"
6. If vendor NOT found: invoke stuck agent ("Unknown vendor: [name]. Create new vendor entry?")
```

See the difference? Every step is explicit. There's no guessing involved.

**Conditional Logic in Directives:**

Real business processes aren't linear - they have branches and decisions. Your Directive needs to account for this. Here's how to handle conditional logic:

```markdown
## Process
1. Read incoming email subject line
2. Classify intent:
   - If subject contains "invoice" or "payment" → Route to invoicing workflow
   - If subject contains "support" or "help" or "issue" → Route to support triage
   - If subject contains "quote" or "pricing" or "interested" → Route to sales pipeline
   - If none of the above → Route to general inbox
3. For each routed email, apply the corresponding sub-directive
```

This gives the AI a decision tree to follow. Notice how specific the trigger words are - "invoice" or "payment" - not vague rules like "if it seems financial."

**Error Handling in Directives:**

Don't just describe the happy path. Tell the AI what to do when things go wrong. Real-world systems fail in predictable ways: APIs time out, files don't exist, data is malformed. Your Directive should anticipate these scenarios.

Here's a robust error handling section:

```markdown
## Error Handling
- If target URL returns 403 (Forbidden): Wait 60 seconds and retry once. If still 403, invoke stuck agent with message "Access denied to [URL]. Check if IP is blocked or credentials expired."
- If data extraction finds < 10 results: Log warning "Low result count: [X] results found" and continue (the directory may have limited listings this week).
- If data extraction finds 0 results: STOP immediately and invoke stuck agent with message "Zero results extracted. Possible page structure change or scraping block."
- If email validation fails for > 20% of records: STOP and invoke stuck agent with message "High invalid email rate: [X]%. Data source may be corrupted."
```

Notice the graduated response. Some errors are warnings (continue with logging), some trigger retries (wait and try again), and some are fatal (stop and escalate to human). This prevents the AI from blindly proceeding when something is seriously wrong.

#### Component 4: Definition of Done

Without a Definition of Done, the AI decides when it's "finished" - and its definition might not match yours. This is like telling a contractor to remodel your kitchen and never specifying what "done" means. They might think "done" is when the cabinets are installed; you might think "done" is when the cabinets are installed, painted, hardware attached, and everything cleaned up.

The Definition of Done is your quality control checkpoint. It tells the AI (and the Tester agent) exactly what success looks like.

**Writing Measurable Definitions:**

- **Bad:** "The form works" → How do you verify this? What does "works" mean?
- **Good:** "The form accepts name, email, and message inputs. Submissions appear in the Supabase 'contacts' table within 5 seconds. A confirmation email is sent to the submitter. The form displays a success message after submission."

**Three Types of Done Criteria:**

1. **Output criteria:** "A CSV file exists at ./output/leads.csv with 50+ rows and no duplicate email addresses"
2. **Quality criteria:** "All email addresses pass format validation (contains @ and valid domain). All phone numbers are 10 digits. Zero rows with empty required fields."
3. **Visual criteria:** "The page renders correctly at desktop (1280x720) and mobile (375x667) viewports. All buttons are clickable. Text is readable at both sizes."

Here's a complete Definition of Done for a lead generation workflow:

```markdown
## Definition of Done
### File Output
- File exists at ./output/brisbane-agents.csv
- File contains 50+ rows (not counting header)
- All rows have unique email addresses (no duplicates)

### Data Quality
- All email addresses pass format validation (regex: ^[^\s@]+@[^\s@]+\.[^\s@]+$)
- All phone numbers are formatted (04XX XXX XXX) or marked as "Not Available"
- Agency name field is never empty
- Active Listings count is a number >= 5

### Process Verification
- Scraping completed within 30 minutes
- Rate limit respected (logs confirm 2-second delays between requests)
- No 403 or 429 errors in execution log
- Final row count matches expected pagination (if 50 results expected, all pages were scraped)
```

**The Definition of Done is the Tester's Checklist:**

When the Tester agent verifies the Coder's work, it checks EVERY item in the Definition of Done. If any item fails, the whole task fails. This prevents partial completion from being marked as done.

Think of it like an airline pre-flight checklist. The pilot doesn't get to skip items because "most of them look good." Every single item must be checked before the plane takes off. Your Directives work the same way.

### From Messy to Clean: Complete Walkthrough

Let's take a REAL business process and show the full translation from messy reality to clean Directive. We'll use client onboarding - something every service business does, and most do inconsistently.

#### The Messy Version (What Exists Today):

"When we get a new client, Sarah sends them the welcome email from her personal template. Then someone (usually Dave) sets up their account in the system. We try to schedule the kickoff call within a week but sometimes it slips. Oh and we need to send them the NDA - that's in the shared drive somewhere. I think it's in the Legal folder? Anyway, once they sign it, we can get started. We also need to give them access to the project folder, but that depends on what package they bought."

Sound familiar? This is how most businesses actually operate. Tribal knowledge, multiple people doing different parts, vague timelines, missing documentation.

#### Step 1: Identify the Hidden Steps

When we dissect this messy process, here's what we discover:

- **Send welcome email** → WHO sends it? (Sarah) → WHAT template? (her personal template) → WHEN? (after contract signing)
- **Set up account** → WHICH system? (unspecified) → WHAT information is needed? (depends on package) → WHO does it? (usually Dave)
- **Schedule kickoff call** → HOW? (unspecified) → WITHIN what timeframe? ("within a week" = 7 days) → WHO attends? (unspecified)
- **Send NDA** → WHERE is it? ("Legal folder" on shared drive) → HOW is it sent? (unspecified) → WHO signs first? (client)
- **Grant access to project folder** → WHERE? (Google Drive?) → WHAT permissions? (depends on package tier)

Already we can see the gaps. There's no single source of truth, timelines are vague, and responsibilities are unclear.

#### Step 2: The Clean Directive

Now let's transform this into a proper Antigravity Directive:

```markdown
# Client Onboarding Directive

## Objective
Onboard a new client within 24 hours of contract signing. Ensure all account setup, communications, and documents are completed before the kickoff call.

## Inputs
- Client data: Name, company name, email, phone, package tier (Starter/Growth/Enterprise)
- Contract signed date: retrieved from HubSpot deal record
- Credentials: .env[HUBSPOT_KEY], .env[RESEND_KEY], .env[CALENDLY_KEY], .env[DOCUSIGN_KEY]
- Templates:
  - ./templates/welcome-email.html
  - ./templates/nda-template.pdf
  - ./templates/project-folder-structure/

## Process

### Step 1: Send Welcome Email
1. Retrieve client data from HubSpot using deal ID
2. Load welcome-email.html template
3. Personalize template with:
   - Client first name
   - Company name
   - Package tier details
   - Login credentials for client portal (generate temporary password)
   - Calendly scheduling link (embedded in email)
4. Send email via Resend API to client email address
5. CC: assigned account manager (determined by package tier)
6. BCC: onboarding@company.com (for record keeping)
7. Log email sent timestamp to HubSpot timeline

### Step 2: Create HubSpot Customer Record
1. Update deal lifecycle stage from "Closed Won" to "Customer"
2. Populate customer properties:
   - Company name
   - Primary contact (name, email, phone)
   - Package tier
   - Contract start date
   - Annual contract value
   - Next renewal date (start date + 12 months)
3. Assign to account manager based on package tier:
   - Starter: Sarah Johnson
   - Growth: Dave Martinez
   - Enterprise: Regional director (check territory mapping)
4. Create first customer success task: "30-day check-in" scheduled for (start date + 30 days)

### Step 3: Generate and Send NDA
1. Load nda-template.pdf from ./templates/
2. Populate template fields:
   - Client company legal name
   - Client signatory name and title
   - Current date
   - Contract term (match HubSpot contract term)
3. Upload populated PDF to DocuSign
4. Configure signing order:
   - Recipient 1: Client signatory (sign first)
   - Recipient 2: Company director (auto-signs after client)
5. Send DocuSign envelope with subject: "NDA for [Company Name] - Signature Required"
6. Set reminder: 48 hours if not signed
7. Log DocuSign envelope ID to HubSpot

### Step 4: Schedule Kickoff Call
1. Retrieve Calendly link for assigned account manager
2. Generate unique scheduling link with parameters:
   - Duration: 30 minutes
   - Available window: Next 5 business days only
   - Attendees: client + account manager + project lead
   - Meeting title: "[Company Name] Kickoff Call"
3. Calendly link was embedded in welcome email (Step 1)
4. Create HubSpot task for account manager: "Kickoff call preparation" due 24 hours before scheduled time
5. If no meeting scheduled within 3 days, escalate: invoke stuck agent with "Client has not scheduled kickoff: [client name]"

### Step 5: Create Project Workspace
1. Navigate to Google Drive company folder
2. Duplicate appropriate template folder based on package tier:
   - Starter: "Client Template - Starter"
   - Growth: "Client Template - Growth"
   - Enterprise: "Client Template - Enterprise"
3. Rename duplicated folder: "[Company Name] - [Start Date]"
4. Configure folder permissions:
   - Client email: Viewer (view only)
   - Account manager: Editor
   - Project team members: Editor (based on package tier)
5. Copy folder link to HubSpot company record in "Project Folder" field
6. Send folder access notification to client email with message: "Your project workspace is ready"

### Step 6: Create Client Portal Account
1. Navigate to client portal admin (portal.company.com/admin)
2. Create new user account:
   - Email: client email
   - Temporary password: auto-generated (16 characters, alphanumeric + symbols)
   - Company association: link to company record
   - Permission level: determined by package tier
3. Send login credentials via email (already included in welcome email Step 1)
4. Set account flag: "password_reset_required" = true (force password change on first login)

## Error Handling
- If HubSpot API returns 401 (Unauthorized): Check .env[HUBSPOT_KEY] expiration. Invoke stuck agent if expired.
- If client email bounces: Mark deal as "On Hold - Invalid Email" and invoke stuck agent immediately
- If DocuSign envelope fails to send: Retry once after 5 minutes. If still fails, invoke stuck agent with error details
- If Google Drive folder duplication fails: Check Drive storage quota. Invoke stuck agent if quota exceeded
- If Calendly link generation fails: Use fallback manual scheduling link and create task for account manager to schedule manually

## Definition of Done

### Communications Sent
- Welcome email delivered successfully (Resend confirms delivery, no bounce)
- Welcome email opened by client (tracked via Resend)
- DocuSign NDA envelope sent and marked "Sent" status
- Project folder access notification delivered

### Records Created
- HubSpot company record updated with all required fields populated
- HubSpot lifecycle stage = "Customer"
- HubSpot timeline shows welcome email log entry
- DocuSign envelope ID recorded in HubSpot
- Google Drive folder link recorded in HubSpot

### Access Granted
- Client portal account created and credentials sent
- Google Drive folder shared with correct permissions
- Calendly scheduling link available and active

### Timing Requirements
- All steps completed within 4 hours of contract signing
- If kickoff call not scheduled within 3 days, escalation triggered

### Quality Checks
- No missing data fields in HubSpot record
- NDA template populated with correct legal names (no placeholder text remaining)
- Google Drive folder structure matches package tier template
- All email addresses validated before sending (no malformed addresses)
```

#### The Transformation

Let's compare what we started with to what we ended with:

**Before:** A 4-sentence vague description with unstated assumptions, unclear ownership, and no timeline.

**After:** A comprehensive 90-line Directive with:
- 6 major steps broken into 30+ sub-steps
- Specific tools named (HubSpot, Resend, DocuSign, Calendly, Google Drive)
- Conditional logic (package tier determines folder template and account manager)
- Error handling for 5 common failure scenarios
- 15 measurable completion criteria
- 4-hour completion window

The messy version left room for interpretation at every step. The clean Directive leaves room for interpretation at ZERO steps. An AI can execute this exactly as specified, and the Tester can verify every completion criterion.

More importantly, any human employee could also follow this Directive perfectly on their first day. That's the power of proper documentation.

### Modular Design: Building a Directive Library

One of the biggest mistakes people make is trying to write one giant Directive for their entire business. They create a 500-line document called "Business Operations Master SOP" that tries to cover everything from lead generation to invoicing to client offboarding.

**Don't do this.**

Keep your Directives modular. Think of each Directive as a Lego brick - small, focused, and reusable. You can combine multiple Directives to create complex workflows, but each Directive should do ONE thing well.

#### Why Modular Design Matters:

1. **Easier to test:** You can test invoicing without testing onboarding. When you update the lead generation process, you don't risk breaking the content pipeline.

2. **Easier to maintain:** If your invoicing workflow changes (new accounting software), you only update one Directive instead of hunting through a 500-line document.

3. **Easier to self-anneal:** When the invoicing Directive learns that a new vendor sends invoices in a different format, that learning stays contained. It doesn't pollute the onboarding Directive.

4. **Easier to reuse:** The same "send email" sub-directive can be called by onboarding, invoicing, and support workflows. Write once, use everywhere.

5. **Easier to delegate:** Each Directive is a single task for the Coder agent. A 50-line Directive fits comfortably in the agent's context window; a 500-line document might exceed it.

#### Recommended Directive Library Structure:

Here's how to organize your directives folder:

```
directives/
├── lead-generation/
│   ├── scrape-directory.md
│   ├── enrich-leads.md
│   ├── score-and-qualify.md
│   └── export-to-crm.md
├── client-onboarding/
│   ├── welcome-sequence.md
│   ├── account-setup.md
│   ├── kickoff-scheduling.md
│   └── project-workspace-setup.md
├── invoicing/
│   ├── extract-invoice-data.md
│   ├── validate-and-match.md
│   ├── approval-workflow.md
│   └── sync-to-quickbooks.md
├── content-pipeline/
│   ├── research-topic.md
│   ├── write-draft.md
│   ├── edit-and-optimize.md
│   └── publish-and-distribute.md
└── support/
    ├── triage-tickets.md
    ├── auto-respond-faq.md
    └── escalation-routing.md
```

Notice how each folder represents a business function, and each Directive inside represents a specific step within that function. The onboarding workflow might use all four onboarding Directives in sequence, but each one is independently testable and maintainable.

> [!TIP]
> If you find yourself writing "Step 24" in a Directive, it's too long. Split it into two separate workflows.

A good rule of thumb: if your Directive is more than 100 lines or has more than 10 major steps, consider splitting it. You can always create a "meta-directive" that calls multiple sub-directives in sequence.

### Common Directive Mistakes (And How to Fix Them)

After reviewing hundreds of Directives from clients, I've seen the same mistakes repeatedly. Here are the five most common, and how to fix them:

#### Mistake 1: Too Vague

**What it looks like:**
```markdown
## Process
1. Process the data
2. Clean up any errors
3. Send results to the client
```

**Why it's a problem:** Every step is ambiguous. "Process the data" how? "Clean up any errors" - what constitutes an error? "Send results" - via what method?

**How to fix it:**
```markdown
## Process
1. Import CSV file from ./input/raw-data.csv
2. Validate each row:
   - Check that email field matches format: ^[^\s@]+@[^\s@]+\.[^\s@]+$
   - Check that phone field contains exactly 10 digits
   - Check that company field is not empty
3. For any row that fails validation:
   - Log the row number and failure reason to ./logs/validation-errors.log
   - Remove row from dataset
4. Export cleaned dataset to ./output/cleaned-data.csv
5. Send cleaned-data.csv to client via email using template ./templates/data-delivery-email.html
```

#### Mistake 2: Missing Error Handling

**What it looks like:**
```markdown
## Process
1. Scrape product data from e-commerce site
2. Save to database
3. Send completion notification
```

**Why it's a problem:** This only describes the happy path. What happens when the website is down? When the database connection fails? When the site blocks your IP?

**How to fix it:**
```markdown
## Process
1. Scrape product data from e-commerce site URL
2. Save to Supabase products table
3. Send completion notification via email

## Error Handling
- If target site returns 503 (Service Unavailable): Wait 5 minutes and retry up to 3 times. If still unavailable, invoke stuck agent.
- If target site returns 403 (Forbidden): Stop immediately and invoke stuck agent with message "IP may be blocked. Check if VPN/proxy is needed."
- If Supabase insert fails with "duplicate key": This is expected for existing products. Log as warning and continue.
- If Supabase connection timeout: Retry once after 10 seconds. If timeout persists, invoke stuck agent.
- If email notification fails to send: Log error but mark task as complete (don't block on notification failure).
```

#### Mistake 3: Hardcoded Values

**What it looks like:**
```markdown
## Process
1. Scrape page 1 through page 10 of the directory
2. Extract exactly 50 results
3. Save to output.csv
```

**Why it's a problem:** What happens when the directory has 15 pages? Or only 3 pages? Hardcoded values make your Directive brittle.

**How to fix it:**
```markdown
## Process
1. Navigate to directory first page
2. Scrape all available pages:
   - Extract results from current page
   - Check if "Next Page" button exists
   - If yes: click and repeat
   - If no: stop pagination
3. Continue until one of these conditions:
   - No more pages available
   - 100 total results collected (safety limit)
   - 30 minutes elapsed (timeout)
4. Save all collected results to ./output/results-[timestamp].csv
```

#### Mistake 4: No Definition of Done

**What it looks like:**
```markdown
## Objective
Create a contact form

## Process
1. Build HTML form with fields
2. Connect to database
3. Add validation
```

(No Definition of Done section at all)

**Why it's a problem:** How does the AI know when it's finished? How does the Tester know what to verify? This Directive has no measurable completion criteria.

**How to fix it:**
```markdown
## Definition of Done

### Functionality
- Form accepts name (required), email (required), phone (optional), message (required)
- Form validates email format before submission
- Form validates required fields are not empty
- On successful submission: data saved to Supabase contacts table
- On successful submission: confirmation email sent to submitter
- On successful submission: notification email sent to admin@company.com

### User Experience
- Form displays error messages for invalid inputs (red text below field)
- Form displays success message after submission (green banner at top)
- Form clears all fields after successful submission
- Form is accessible via keyboard navigation (tab through fields)

### Technical Requirements
- Form renders correctly at 1280x720 (desktop) and 375x667 (mobile)
- Form submission completes within 3 seconds
- All database writes complete before success message displays
- No console errors in browser developer tools
```

#### Mistake 5: Monolithic Design

**What it looks like:**
A single 200-line Directive titled "Complete Business Automation" that tries to handle lead generation, qualification, onboarding, project management, invoicing, and reporting in one file.

**Why it's a problem:**
- Impossible to test (must test everything at once)
- Impossible to maintain (changing one thing risks breaking everything)
- Exceeds agent context limits
- No reusability across workflows
- Self-annealing updates affect the entire document

**How to fix it:**
Split into focused Directives:

```
directives/
├── lead-generation.md (scraping and enrichment)
├── lead-qualification.md (scoring and filtering)
├── client-onboarding.md (welcome sequence)
├── project-kickoff.md (first meeting setup)
├── invoice-generation.md (create and send invoices)
└── monthly-reporting.md (compile and distribute reports)
```

Then create a meta-directive that orchestrates the sequence:

```markdown
# Business Automation Workflow

## Process
1. Execute lead-generation.md weekly on Mondays
2. Execute lead-qualification.md on any leads with status "New"
3. When lead status changes to "Customer", execute client-onboarding.md
4. After onboarding complete, execute project-kickoff.md
5. Execute invoice-generation.md monthly on 1st of month
6. Execute monthly-reporting.md on last day of month
```

Now each component is independently testable, maintainable, and reusable.

### Try It Yourself: Your First Directive in 15 Minutes

Theory is useful, but practice is essential. Let's write your first Directive right now. Pick the most repetitive task in your business - something you do weekly or monthly that feels like it could be automated.

**Step 1: Pick Your Task (1 minute)**

Common examples:
- Weekly sales pipeline review
- Monthly invoice processing
- Lead follow-up emails
- Social media post scheduling
- Report generation and distribution

For this exercise, let's say you picked: "Weekly lead follow-up."

**Step 2: Write Your Objective (2 minutes)**

Open a text file and write the Objective section. Remember the Specificity Test from earlier. Be precise about the outcome.

```markdown
# Weekly Lead Follow-Up Directive

## Objective
Every Monday morning, review all leads in HubSpot with status "New" from the previous week and send personalized follow-up emails to leads who have not responded to the initial outreach.
```

**Step 3: List Your Inputs (3 minutes)**

What data sources, credentials, and configuration does this task need?

```markdown
## Inputs
- Data source: HubSpot CRM, leads table
- Date filter: Created date within last 7 days
- Status filter: Status = "New" AND Last contacted > 3 days ago
- Credentials: .env[HUBSPOT_KEY], .env[EMAIL_API_KEY]
- Email template: ./templates/lead-followup.html
- Sender email: sales@yourcompany.com
```

**Step 4: Write Your Process (6 minutes)**

For each process step, ask yourself: "If I told a brand new employee to do this, would they know EXACTLY what to do?" If the answer is no, add more detail.

```markdown
## Process
1. Query HubSpot for leads matching criteria:
   - Status = "New"
   - Created date >= 7 days ago
   - Last activity date >= 3 days ago (no recent contact)
2. For each lead retrieved:
   - Extract: first name, email, company name, original inquiry topic
   - Check if lead has opened previous emails (use HubSpot email tracking)
3. Segment leads into two groups:
   - Group A: Opened previous email but didn't reply (warm leads)
   - Group B: Never opened any email (cold leads)
4. For Group A leads:
   - Load ./templates/lead-followup-warm.html
   - Personalize with first name and reference their inquiry topic
   - Send via email API with subject: "Quick follow-up: [inquiry topic]"
5. For Group B leads:
   - Load ./templates/lead-followup-cold.html
   - Personalize with first name
   - Send via email API with subject: "Checking in: [company name]"
6. Log all sent emails to HubSpot timeline
7. Update lead last activity date to today
```

**Step 5: Define "Done" (3 minutes)**

How do you know this task completed successfully?

```markdown
## Definition of Done
- All qualifying leads from previous week processed (0 leads remaining with matching criteria)
- Follow-up emails sent to 100% of Group A leads
- Follow-up emails sent to 100% of Group B leads
- All sent emails logged to HubSpot timeline with timestamp
- All lead records updated with new last activity date
- Email delivery rate > 95% (less than 5% bounce rate)
- Execution log contains count of emails sent by segment (Group A: X, Group B: Y)
```

**Step 6: Review and Refine (remaining time)**

Read through your Directive and ask:
- Is every step crystal clear?
- Did I specify WHICH tool to use at each step?
- Did I handle errors and edge cases?
- Is my Definition of Done measurable?

Show your Directive to a colleague who isn't familiar with your process. Ask them: "Could you follow these instructions exactly as written?" If they have questions, your Directive needs more detail.

Congratulations - you've just written your first business playbook entry.

### Key Takeaway

A Directive is your business process, written so precisely that an AI can follow it with zero interpretation. The four components - Objective, Inputs, Process, and Definition of Done - transform vague "how we do things" tribal knowledge into a repeatable, testable, self-improving system.

Your Directive IS your competitive advantage. Most businesses have their processes locked in people's heads or buried in outdated documents. When you have crystal-clear Directives, you have:

- **Consistency:** The process runs the same way every time
- **Scalability:** New team members (human or AI) can execute immediately
- **Quality:** The Definition of Done ensures nothing slips through
- **Improvement:** Self-annealing means the system gets better over time
- **Transferability:** Your business becomes less dependent on any single person

In the next chapter, we'll take these Directives and bring them to life by building the specialized agents that execute them. But remember: the agent is only as good as the Directive it follows. Get this phase right, and everything else becomes easier.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Before moving to Phase 3, ensure you have at least ONE complete Directive written for your most critical workflow. Don't try to document everything at once. Start with the process that causes you the most pain or takes the most time. Once you see that first Directive working flawlessly, you'll be motivated to write the rest.
